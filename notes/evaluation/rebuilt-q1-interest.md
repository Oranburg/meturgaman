> What does Jewish law say about lending at interest, and does it apply to a business loan?

---

Here is what the sources say, each fetched with its edition. Hebrew terms are romanized under SBL general style (the tool's default; no flags fired on the pointed forms).

## The prohibition itself

The Torah states it three times, and the tradition reads each statement as adding something:

- **Exodus 22:24** (Miqra according to the Masorah, CC-BY-SA; JPS Gender-Sensitive Tanakh, CC-BY-NC): "If you lend money to My people, to the poor among you... exact no interest [*neshekh*] from them."
- **Leviticus 25:35–37** (same editions): the context is the impoverished kinsman — "do not exact advance or accrued interest [*neshekh* / *tarbit*], but fear your God."
- **Deuteronomy 23:20–21** (same editions): interest in "money or food or anything else" is forbidden toward a fellow Israelite, but permitted toward a foreigner. Sifrei Devarim 263 records a dispute over whether "you may deduct interest from the foreigner" is a positive commandment or mere permission — the sources disagree and I am reporting both readings.

Rashi on Exodus 22:24 (fetched via the topic sources) explains the word *neshekh* as "biting": like a snakebite, the wound seems small and then swells.

## How the law defines and extends it

- **Mishnah Bava Metzia 5:1** (Torat Emet 357, public domain; Davidson English, CC-BY-NC) defines *neshekh* — lending a sela to receive five dinars — and *tarbit*, increase in commodities.
- **Bava Metzia 63b:10** (Davidson Vocalized Aramaic and English, both CC-BY-NC) gives Rav Naḥman's governing principle: *kol agar naṭar leih asur* — any payment for waiting, any premium for the delay in repayment, is forbidden. This is why the prohibition sweeps far beyond a stated interest rate: disguised premiums, discounts for prepayment, and reciprocal favors can all fall within it (the Gemara at Bava Metzia 75b even extends, rabbinically, to non-monetary benefits).
- **Mishnah Bava Metzia 5:11** (Torat Emet; Davidson): the transgressors are not only the lender — "the lender, the borrower, the guarantor, and the witnesses; and the Rabbis say, also the scribe." Both sides of the transaction violate the law.
- **Shulchan Arukh, Yoreh De'ah 160:1** (Ashlei Ravrevei, Lemberg 1888, public domain) opens the laws of *ribbit* by repeating that list, and the Rema's gloss there is directly on your question: **"there is no distinction between lending to a poor person or to a rich one."**

## So: does it apply to a business loan?

**Yes, as a loan.** Although Exodus and Leviticus speak of the poor, the codified law (Rema on Yoreh De'ah 160:1, above) applies the prohibition to any loan between Jews, including a loan to a wealthy merchant for commercial purposes. The category is the loan structure, not the borrower's need.

**But an investment is not a loan.** The sources distinguish sharply between debt and equity:

- **Mishnah Bava Metzia 5:4** (Torat Emet; Davidson): one may finance a storekeeper for half the profits — but only if the financier genuinely shares the risk and compensates the storekeeper for his labor (pays him "as a worker"); otherwise the storekeeper's unpaid effort on the financier's share is itself disguised interest.
- **Shulchan Arukh, Yoreh De'ah 177:2** (Ashlei Ravrevei, Lemberg 1888): every *‘isqa* — a commercial financing arrangement — is legally "half loan, half deposit" (*palga milveh palga piqadon*). The recipient trades with the funds; half is his debt at his risk, half remains the investor's property at the investor's risk, and the investor must pay something for the recipient's work on the deposit half, or the arrangement collapses into interest.

This *‘isqa* structure is the basis of the **heter iska**, the standard document under which observant Jews and Israeli banks conduct interest-bearing-looking business lending today: the "loan" is recast as part-investment, so the financier's return is profit on his own capital-at-risk rather than payment for waiting. I should flag that this last paragraph is historical characterization on my part — the *‘isqa* texts above are fetched, but I have not fetched a *heter iska* document itself, and the drafting details (e.g., the verification clauses that make the borrower's payment obligation practically fixed) vary by community and are debated among later authorities. If you want, I can search for the later halakhic literature on the heter iska specifically.

**Summary.** Jewish law forbids any premium for the use of money between Jews, on both lender and borrower, regardless of whether the loan is for consumption or commerce. A business financing arrangement is permitted only when structured as genuine risk-sharing — the financier must stand to lose — and the *heter iska* is the traditional instrument for doing exactly that.
