The English edition has no text at 75b:14; that "gap" is just the untranslated chapter colophon, *hadran alakh Eizehu Neshekh*, confirmed in three witnesses (Davidson vocalized, Davidson unvocalized, Wikisource CC-BY-SA). So 75b is a seam: chapter 5 (*Eizehu Neshekh*) ends at segment 14, and chapter 6 (*HaSokher et haUmanin*) opens at 15.

## The mishnah's closing moves (75b:1–4)

The chapter's final mishnah ends with a sequence of widenings — each one pushing *ribbit* past coin:

1. **Interest in labor** (75b:1). Neighbors may exchange equal work-days, but "he may not say to him: Plow with me in the dry season and I will plow with you in the rainy season." A rainy-season day is worth more; the surplus is interest paid in sweat.
2. **Interest in time** (75b:2). Rabban Gamliel adds *ribbit mukdemet* and *ribbit me'ucheret* — a gift sent *before* a hoped-for loan, or *after* repayment "for your money, which was idle with me." Interest need not travel with the loan at all.
3. **Interest in words** (75b:3). Rabbi Shimon: even *verbal* interest — the borrower volunteering useful information ("so-and-so has come from such and such a place") is paying with speech.
4. **Interest as shared guilt** (75b:4). Lender, borrower, guarantor, and witnesses all transgress; the Rabbis add the scribe. Five verses are stacked up, including "you shall not place a stumbling block before the blind" (Lev. 19:14).

Notice the architecture: substance widens (money → labor → words), time widens (before and after the loan), and responsibility widens (from lender to everyone at the table).

## How the gemara builds on it (75b:5–13)

**It ratifies the boldest claim first.** A baraita of Rabbi Shimon ben Yoḥai (75b:5) grounds verbal interest in a wordplay: "*neshekh* of any *davar*" (Deut. 23:20) is read as *dibbur* — even greeting a lender you never used to greet is forbidden. The mishnah's most counterintuitive extension gets a scriptural anchor before anything else.

**Then it does the legal bookkeeping.** Abaye (75b:6) takes the mishnah's undifferentiated list of violators and maps each party to specific verses: the lender violates all of them; the borrower three; guarantor and witnesses only "do not place interest upon him" (Ex. 22:24). The mishnah said *who*; Abaye supplies *which* and *why*.

**Then it turns from law to ethos.** A second baraita of Rabbi Shimon (75b:7): those who lend at interest "lose more than they gain," and — in the gemara's pointed euphemism — they make out that Moses was a scholar and his Torah true, i.e. the reverse: "had Moses our teacher known that there was a profit involved, he would not have written it." Usury is recast as contempt for the Torah itself.

**Then the sugya slides from interest to the creditor relationship as such.** Rav Dimi (75b:8): one who knows his debtor cannot pay may not even *walk past him* — from "do not be to him as a creditor" (Ex. 22:24). Rabbi Ami and Rabbi Asi (75b:9) escalate: doing so is sentencing him to two punishments, "fire and water" (Ps. 66:12). The prohibition on extracting value has become a prohibition on inflicting shame.

**Then the mirror image: prudence toward oneself.** Rav Yehuda in Rav's name (75b:10): lending *without witnesses* violates the stumbling-block verse — you tempt the borrower to deny the debt; Reish Lakish adds that such a lender brings a curse on himself (Ps. 31:19). The same verse that condemned the usurer's enablers in the mishnah now condemns the careless *honest* lender.

**A story tests the principle** (75b:11). Rav Ashi probes whether Ravina really lives by the sages' directives, asking for a ten-dinar loan at the busiest hour before Shabbat. Ravina demands witnesses and a document. "Even I?" — "All the more so": a master absorbed in study is the most likely to forget. The rule survives its hardest case, applied *upward*, to the greatest of men.

**A closing baraita generalizes** (75b:12–13). Three cry out and are not answered: one who lends without witnesses, one who acquires a master for himself, one whose wife rules over him. The gemara then gives three readings of "acquires a master" — attributing property to a gentile, deeding property to one's children in one's lifetime, or staying in a town where one's fortune is bad — left as alternatives, unharmonized.

## The shape of the whole

The chapter on interest ends by dissolving its own boundaries. The mishnah pushes interest beyond money; the gemara pushes beyond interest altogether, into the ethics of holding power over a debtor — and then turns the lens around, onto the lender's duty of prudence toward himself. It descends from verse-derivation (5–6) through homiletic (7–9) to practical counsel (10), narrative (11), and proverb (12–13): a deliberate cadence from law to life at a chapter's close. Then *hadran alakh Eizehu Neshekh* (75b:14), and the new mishnah (75b:15–16) opens a different world — hired artisans, deception, and retraction — where grievance, not prohibition, is the operative category.
