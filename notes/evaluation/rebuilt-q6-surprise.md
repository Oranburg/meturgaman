Here is the find: not a source about contracts, but a line of authority in which **merchant custom itself becomes the mode of contract formation — and then overrides black-letter doctrine that says the contract is impossible to form**. It is the halakhic *lex mercatoria*, and it runs five sources deep. Everything below was fetched, not remembered; edition and licence noted for each.

**1. The dictum — Bava Metzia 74a:3, 5** (Davidson Edition, vocalized Aramaic + English, via Sefaria/Koren, CC-BY-NC):

> אָמַר רַב פַּפִּי מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרָבָא: הַאי סִיטוּמְתָּא – קָנְיָא ... וְהִלְכְתָא לְקַבּוֹלֵי עֲלֵיהּ ״מִי שֶׁפָּרַע״. וּבְאַתְרָא דִּנְהִיגוּ לְמִקְנֵי מַמָּשׁ – קָנוּ.

Merchants marked purchased barrels with a *situmta* (a seal — Jastrow and the Arukh both gloss it) without performing any formal *kinyan*. Baseline rule: the mark only subjects a retracting party to the *mi she-para* imprecation — a purely reputational-moral remedy for breach, no legal enforcement (a category your remedies students would enjoy on its own). But the closing clause is the payload: *where local custom treats it as acquiring outright, it acquires*. Trade usage is not evidence of intent, as in UCC 1-303 — it *constitutes* the formality. Codified at Shulchan Arukh, Choshen Mishpat 201:1 (Lemberg 1898 ed., public domain), which extends it to handshakes and key-delivery, "and so everything merchants customarily use to close."

**2. The escalation — can custom do what doctrine says is impossible?** Halakhah holds *ein adam makneh davar she-lo ba la-olam* — one cannot convey a thing not yet in existence. No futures, no after-acquired property. Then:

- **Tashbetz Katan 398:1** (Warsaw 1902 ed., public domain), in the name of Maharam of Rothenburg: a father who promises his *unborn* son to a friend to serve as mohel or sandak is bound — the honor doesn't exist yet, the child doesn't exist yet, but "since it is the custom of people to pledge their sons to one another... and they keep it," it binds. Custom validates a contract over a nonexistent res.
- **Teshuvot HaRosh 13:20** (Vilna 1881 ed., public domain): the fetched text opens with a community that *sold its right to collect Reuven's future taxes* to another community — a receivables assignment — and the Rosh (as the Chatam Sofer and Pitchei Teshuva both read him) holds that where custom is to convey by lease-deeds things not yet in existence, *situmta* makes it effective.
- **Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat 66** (Pressburg 1855–64 ed., public domain): generalizes it — custom cures both *asmakhta* (the penalty-clause/speculative-condition defect, citing the engagement-penalty custom from Tosafot BM 66a) *and* the nonexistent-subject-matter defect.

**3. The dispute — do not let anyone tell you this is settled.** Pitchei Teshuva on CM 201:2 (Lemberg 1898 ed.) records R. Akiva Eiger (Responsa, no. 134) citing the Yam Shel Shlomo and Hagahot Mordechai to Shabbat: *situmta* works only for things already in existence, because it merely substitutes for a formal *kinyan* — it cannot manufacture capacity the law never gave. So the tradition splits on exactly the question you would frame it as: is trade usage a *substitute formality* (limited by underlying doctrine) or an *independent source of obligation* (which overrides it)?

Why this is worth your time specifically: it is Karl Llewellyn's argument — that merchant practice should supply the law of formation, not just interpretation — conducted from the 13th century (Maharam, d. 1293) to the 19th (Chatam Sofer, R. Akiva Eiger), with the Rosh's tax-receivables case doing the work Grant Gilmore's after-acquired-property story does for Article 9. Contemporary poskim run click-wrap and credit-card assent through this exact channel. If you want next steps, `meturgaman chain "Bava Metzia 74a:3"` lists the full transmission (Rif, Rambam Sales 7:6, Tur/SA CM 201, eleven responsa); the R. Akiva Eiger responsum no. 134 would be the natural thing to fetch next.
