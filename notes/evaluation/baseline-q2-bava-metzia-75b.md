# Baseline, question 2

Asked 2026-08-06, before any change to the agent. The question, verbatim:

> Walk me through the sugya at Bava Metzia 75b. How is the argument built?

The agent's answer, unedited:

---

I fetched the full amud in two editions — the William Davidson Talmud, vocalized Aramaic and English (Sefaria, via the Noe Edition Koren Talmud Bavli; CC-BY-NC, both versions flagged as locked, so I will quote briefly and paraphrase the rest). The `sugya` command confirms the unit is the whole amud, and structurally it is the closing sugya of chapter 5, *Eizehu Neshekh*: the *hadran* falls at 75b:14, and 75b:15 already begins the next chapter's mishnah on hiring craftsmen.

Here is how the argument is built. It is a widening spiral: the prohibition of interest keeps expanding — from money, to labor, to time, to speech — and then the gemara pivots from law to the ethics of being a creditor at all, ending in pure aggadah.

## The mishnah (75b:1–4): interest expands in three directions

1. **From money to labor** (75b:1). The mishnah is finishing a case of reciprocal work: one may not say "plow with me in the dry season and I will plow with you in the rainy season," because rainy-season plowing is harder — repaying easy labor with hard labor is interest paid in work, not coin.
2. **From the loan itself to the time around it** (75b:2). Rabban Gamliel: there is *ribbit mukdemet* and *ribbit me'uḥeret* — a gift sent before the loan ("so that you will lend me") or after repayment ("for your money that sat idle with me"). Interest need not travel inside the transaction; gratitude with a price tag counts.
3. **From things to words** (75b:3). Rabbi Shimon: there is *ribbit devarim*, verbal interest — the borrower may not even pass useful information to the lender ("know that so-and-so has arrived from such-and-such a place").
4. **The cast of violators** (75b:4). Lender, borrower, guarantor, and witnesses all transgress; the Rabbis add the scribe. The mishnah then stacks up five verses — Lev 25:37, Lev 25:36, Exod 22:24 (twice: "do not be to him as a creditor" and "do not place *neshekh* upon him"), and Lev 19:14, the stumbling block before the blind.

## The gemara (75b:5–7): warrant, bookkeeping, and a moral verdict

- **The exegetical warrant for verbal interest** (75b:5). A baraita of Rabbi Shimon ben Yoḥai: a debtor who never used to greet his creditor may not begin greeting him now. The prooftext is Deut 23:20, *neshekh kol davar* — "interest of any *davar* (thing/word)" — read as *dibbur*: "even speech is forbidden." The wordplay is the hinge; it retroactively grounds the mishnah's boldest expansion.
- **Assigning the verses to the parties** (75b:6). Abaye does the legal bookkeeping for the mishnah's list: the lender violates *all* of the verses; the borrower violates "you shall not cause your brother to take interest" plus *lifnei iver* (he creates the lender's sin); the guarantor and witnesses violate only "do not place interest upon him." The five verses were not rhetorical pile-up — each attaches to someone.
- **The peroration on lenders** (75b:7). A baraita of Rabbi Shimon: those who lend at interest "lose more than they gain," and moreover they "make Moses our teacher wise and his Torah true" — a scribal euphemism, inverted: their conduct declares the opposite, saying that had Moses known there was profit in it, he would never have written the prohibition. The Koren translation flags the euphemism explicitly. Usury is recast as heresy-in-practice.

## The pivot (75b:8–11): from interest to the ethics of the creditor

Having exhausted interest proper, the sugya widens once more — now to creditor conduct with no interest involved at all.

- **Rav Dimi** (75b:8), arriving from the Land of Israel: a creditor who knows his debtor cannot pay may not even walk past him — derived from "do not be to him as a creditor" (Exod 22:24). His mere presence is a dun.
- **Rabbi Ami and Rabbi Asi** (75b:9) intensify: doing so is like sentencing him to two punishments, from Ps 66:12 — "we went through fire and through water."
- **Rav and Reish Lakish** (75b:10) turn to the lender's own prudence: lending without witnesses violates *lifnei iver* (Rav — you tempt the borrower to deny the debt) or "brings a curse upon himself" (Reish Lakish, from Ps 31:19 — when you claim without proof, people will call you the liar).
- **The test narrative** (75b:11). The Sages tell Rav Ashi that Ravina fulfills everything the Sages say, so Rav Ashi tests him at the worst possible moment — the eve of Shabbat toward sunset — asking for ten *zuz* for a land purchase. Ravina: bring witnesses and we will write a document. Rav Ashi: "even me?" Ravina: *all the more so* you — a master absorbed in his learning is the likeliest to forget, and I would bring the curse on myself. The story dramatizes that the rule is principled, not a suspicion of bad character.

## The close (75b:12–13): dissolution into aggadah

A final baraita: three cry out and are not answered — the one who lends without witnesses, the one who acquires a master over himself, and the one whose wife rules over him. The gemara then gives three anonymous readings (*ikka de-amrei*) of "acquires a master": one who attributes his property to a gentile, one who deeds his property to his children in his lifetime, and one who fares badly in one town yet will not move to another. Characteristically for a chapter's end, the sugya decrescendos from halakhah into proverb, leaves the three readings side by side without deciding, and then: *Hadran alakh Eizehu Neshekh* (75b:14).

**The shape of the whole:** mishnaic expansion of *ribbit* (coin → labor → time → speech) → exegetical grounding of the furthest expansion (*davar*/*dibbur*) → precise legal allocation (Abaye) → theological condemnation (R. Shimon) → generalization from interest to creditor ethics (Rav Dimi) → prudential rules tested by story (Ravina) → folk-wisdom coda. One key romanization note: the term is *ribbit* (so pointed in the Davidson edition, רִבִּית), under SBL general — you will also meet it as *ribbis* in Ashkenazi usage.
