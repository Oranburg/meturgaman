Here is what I found for you: **situmta** — Bava Metzia 74a, the sugya where the Talmud lets merchant custom *manufacture a mode of contract formation that the formal law does not contain*.

## The source

**Bava Metzia 74a:3–5** (William Davidson Edition – Vocalized Aramaic, via Sefaria, CC-BY-NC):

> אָמַר רַב פַּפִּי מִשְּׁמֵיהּ דְּרָבָא: הַאי סִיטוּמְתָּא – קָנְיָא

Rav Pappi in Rava's name: "this situmta — it acquires." Rashi ad loc. (Vilna edition, public domain) explains the situmta as the merchant's seal-mark chalked on wine barrels bought in bulk and left in the seller's cellar — a gesture with zero standing in the formal taxonomy of kinyanim (meshikhah, hagbahah, chalipin...). The sugya then splits: Rav Chaviva says the mark effects *actual* acquisition; the Rabbis say it only subjects the reneging party to the **mi shepara** curse. The holding:

> וְהִלְכְתָא לְקַבּוֹלֵי עֲלֵיהּ ״מִי שֶׁפָּרַע״. וּבְאַתְרָא דִּנְהִיגוּ לְמִקְנֵי מַמָּשׁ – קָנוּ
> *u-ve'atra dinhigu lemiqnei mammash – qanu* (SBL general-purpose)

"And in a place where the custom is to acquire outright by it — they acquire." Codified at **Mishneh Torah, Sales 7:6** (Torat Emet ed.) and **Shulchan Arukh, Choshen Mishpat 201** (Lemberg 1898, public domain).

## Why this should surprise a contracts scholar

Two things, and the second is the one you would not have gone looking for:

1. **Custom here is generative, not interpretive.** In your world, trade usage (UCC 1-303) fills gaps in a contract that already exists. Situmta is stronger: custom *creates the formality itself*. Halakhah — famously formalist about acquisition — builds in an internal lex mercatoria. And the fallback position is just as rich: mi shepara is a purely reputational-religious sanction enforcing executory deals the law leaves revocable. If you know Lisa Bernstein's diamond-industry work on extralegal sanctions, the Rabbis' view in this sugya is a 5th-century instance of the same architecture.

2. **The Chatam Sofer's escalation.** My search surfaced **Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat 66**, where he argues that where custom governs, situmta validates even an **asmakhta** — the penalty clause halakhah normally voids for lack of true assent (the doctrinal cousin of your penalty-clause rule) — and even acquisition of a **davar shelo ba la'olam**, a thing not yet in existence (compare future goods, UCC 2-105(2)). So custom does not merely add a formality to the approved list; it overrides *substantive incapacities* of the contract system. This is the chain modern poskim ride to validate handshakes, signatures, and electronic contracting.
