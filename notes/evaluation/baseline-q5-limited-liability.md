# Baseline, question 5

Asked 2026-08-06, before any change to the agent. The question, verbatim:

> Is there Jewish wisdom about limited liability?

The agent's answer, unedited:

---

Yes — though the honest answer is that the tradition starts from the *opposite* default and then builds limited liability as an exception. Here is what the sources actually say, each fetched with its edition.

**1. The baseline is unlimited personal liability.**
Mishnah Bava Batra 10:8 (Torat Emet 357, public domain; William Davidson English, CC-BY-NC): "One who lends money to another by means of a promissory note can collect the debt from liened property" — even property the debtor has since sold to third parties. A debt in halakhah creates a lien (*shi'bud*) on everything the borrower owns. There is no corporate veil in the classical sources; the person is the firm.

**2. But liability can be contractually limited to a single asset — non-recourse lending.**
The device is the *apoteke mefurash*, the explicitly designated collateral. Bava Metzia 66b:11 (William Davidson Vocalized Aramaic, CC-BY-NC): Rav Pappa's formula is *lo yehe lekha pera'on ella mi-zo* — "you shall have payment only from this." The same formula appears at Bava Metzia 15b:5 and 110b:10. The Geonim spelled out the consequence (Teshuvot HaGeonim, Harkavy 465): if that formula was said, the creditor cannot reach the debtor's other assets *even if a river washes the field away* — the loss falls on the lender. The Shulchan Arukh codifies the distinction at Choshen Mishpat 117:1 (Lemberg 1898 edition, public domain): an ordinary *apoteke* still lets the creditor pursue other assets if the collateral is destroyed; only the explicit "from here you shall collect" formula caps recourse. That is limited liability by contract, roughly fifteen centuries before the modern corporation.

**3. The *iska*: engineered risk-splitting for investment capital.**
Bava Metzia 104b:14 (Davidson Aramaic/English, CC-BY-NC): "The Nehardeans say: this *iska* is half loan and half deposit; the Sages enacted a matter that is agreeable to the borrower and agreeable to the lender." The investor bears the risk on the deposit half; the managing partner bears it only on the loan half — each side's downside is deliberately capped. This structure is the ancestor of the *heter iska* used to this day in Israeli and Orthodox banking.

**4. Guarantors have secondary, ordered liability.**
The same Mishnah (Bava Batra 10:8) rules that one who lends through a guarantor may not collect from the guarantor first — the creditor must exhaust the debtor. The Talmudic development runs through Bava Batra 173a–174b (the curated source list for the Laws of Contracts and Guarantors). Liability attaches in a defined order and can be shaped by stipulation (the *arev kablan* accepts primary liability; the plain *arev* does not).

**5. Statutory floors under the debtor — collection itself is limited.**
- Deuteronomy 24:10–11 (Miqra according to the Masorah, CC-BY-SA; JPS Gender-Sensitive, CC-BY-NC): "you must not enter the house to seize the pledge" — the creditor waits outside.
- Bava Metzia 113b:6 (Davidson, CC-BY-NC): *mesadrin le-va'al chov* — the court "arranges" for the debtor, leaving him necessary utensils and basic subsistence. An exemption regime, in modern terms.
- Deuteronomy 15:1–2 (JPS): "Every seventh year you shall practice remission of debts" — a periodic systemic discharge, functionally the ancestor of the fresh start.

**6. The modern corporation is a live halakhic question, not a settled one.**
A search for *be-eravon mugbal* ("Ltd.") turned up Peninei Halakhah, Kashrut 31:6:5, which treats a gentile-owned limited-liability company as genuinely owned by its shareholders for immersion-of-vessels purposes "even though their ownership is limited." That is one contemporary position; the broader responsa literature on whether shareholders "own" corporate assets (for interest, chametz, Shabbat) is divided, and I did not find those responsa on Sefaria to quote — so I will note the debate exists rather than reconstruct it from memory.

**The shape of the wisdom:** halakhah refused to make limited liability the default — a person answers for their debts with everything, which the tradition saw as a matter of honesty (*peri'at ba'al chov mitzvah*, the Talmud's dictum that repaying is a commandment). But it permitted liability to be limited *openly and by agreement* — the apoteke formula must be said aloud, the iska split is a named enactment — and it imposed hard limits on what a creditor may do to a human being. Limitation by transparent contract, yes; limitation by silence, no. That distinction is arguably the tradition's most pointed contribution to how a law professor might think about the corporate veil.
