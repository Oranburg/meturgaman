# Baseline, question 3

Asked 2026-08-06, before any change to the agent. The question, verbatim:

> Rambam and Ravad disagree about repentance. Where, and what turns on it?

The agent's answer, unedited:

---

They disagree in nine places across Hilkhot Teshuvah itself — Ravad glossed chapters 2, 3 (twice), 4, 5, 6, 8 (twice), and 10, and left chapters 1, 7, and 9 untouched. All texts below were fetched via Sefaria: Hebrew from the ToratEmet edition (Public Domain), English from Simon Glazer's 1927 translation of the hassagot (Public Domain) and Eliyahu Touger's translation of the Rambam (Moznaim, CC-BY-NC). Three of the disputes are doctrinal battles; the rest are smaller but still instructive.

## The three big ones

**1. Foreknowledge and free will — Teshuvah 5:5.** Rambam raises the classic problem (if God knows I will be wicked, how am I free?), answers that God's knowledge is His essence and therefore beyond human comprehension, and tells the reader to hold both truths. Ravad's hassagah opens with the sharpest methodological rebuke in the set: "לא נהג זה המחבר מנהג החכמים... החל בשאלות קושיות והניח הדבר בקושיא והחזירו לאמונה" — "This one did not follow the custom of scholars; for no man begins a thing which he knows not how to finish... he commenced with inquiries and questions and left the matter under question and turned it around to faith" (Glazer). Better, Ravad says, to have left the innocent in their innocence than to plant a doubt you cannot resolve. He then offers his own "partial answer": since God ceded moral agency to man, His knowledge is not a decree — it is like an astrologer's knowledge of what the stars incline a person toward, which the intellect can still escape. And then, remarkably, he ends: **וכל זה איננו שוה** — "even this is not a comparison." He concedes his own answer fails too. What turns on it: whether teshuvah is coherent at all, and — the real quarrel — whether a codifier should expose ordinary readers to a question he admits he cannot answer.

**2. The Egypt problem — Teshuvah 6:5.** Rambam explains why the Egyptians were punished despite the decree "and they shall serve them, and they shall afflict them": the decree named no individual. Ravad calls this "אריכות דברים שאינן מתובלים... כמעט אני אומר שהם דברי נערות" — "unseasoned, lengthy words... I am tempted to say, they are childish words" — because the sinners could retort: on whom then did Your decree fall, seeing it was fulfilled? His own two answers: God punishes the wicked through the more wicked (Egypt deserved plagues anyway, and Pharaoh's insolence before God's messenger sealed it), and Egypt exceeded the decree — God said "afflict them," they enslaved with rigor and drowned the children ("I was but a little angry, but they helped for evil," Zech. 1:15).

**3. The disembodied world to come — Teshuvah 8:2 and 8:8.** Rambam: "העוה"ב אין בו גוף" — the world to come has no body or form; "the crown on the heads of the righteous" is a parable. Ravad: "דברי האיש הזה בעיני קרובים למי שאומר אין תחיית המתים לגופות אלא לנשמות בלבד" — "This man's words are in my eyes nigh to such who assert that there is no resurrection to body but the souls alone." He stacks up Ketubot 111b (the righteous will rise in their garments), Shabbat 114a (burial instructions given in hope of rising), Sanhedrin 92a and 91b (the righteous do not return to dust; they rise with their blemishes and are healed), and concludes the bodies will be made strong like the angels' and like Elijah's, and the crowns will be literal. At 8:8 he adds that Rambam "נראה כמכחיש" — appears to deny — the world's future renewal ("six thousand years the world endures, and one desolate"). What turns on it is severe by Rambam's own rules: in this very treatise (3:6) one who denies resurrection has no share in the world to come, so Ravad is holding Rambam's criterion up against Rambam. This is the gloss that feeds the resurrection controversy to which Rambam responded in his Treatise on Resurrection.

## The one everyone quotes — 3:7 on the corporealist

Rambam lists five who are called *minim*; the third is "one who accepts one Master, but says He is corporeal and has a form." Ravad: "ולמה קרא לזה מין וכמה גדולים וטובים ממנו הלכו בזו המחשבה לפי מה שראו במקראות ויותר ממה שראו בדברי האגדות המשבשות את הדעות" — why call such a one a heretic? Greater and better men than he held this thought, misled by the verses and still more by aggadot that confound opinions. Note what Ravad does *not* say: he does not defend corporealism as true. The dispute is over whether a good-faith doctrinal error, reached honestly from the sources, excludes a Jew from the world to come. Rambam: dogma has a threshold and error crosses it. Ravad: culpability tracks the sinner, not the proposition.

## The smaller four

- **2:5 — public confession.** Rambam: publicizing sin is praiseworthy only for sins against one's fellow; sins toward God need not be broadcast, and revealing them is brazen. Ravad: "וכן עבירות המפורסמות ומגולות... שכמו שנתפרסם החטא כך צריך לפרסם התשובה ויתבייש ברבים" — sins already public must be repented publicly, even those against Heaven alone; as the sin was published, so must the repentance be. A live practical difference in how confession works.
- **3:2 — when the wicked die.** Rambam reads "sealed at once for death" as: one whose sins outweigh his merits dies immediately in his wickedness. Ravad: "יש רשעים חיים הרבה" — plenty of wicked people live long; "sealed at once" means only that they will not complete their allotted years.
- **4:4 — a reading.** Rambam has "the one who eats the *shor* (ox) of the poor." Ravad: "דומה שהוא שונה שור ברי"ש ואינו אלא שוד בדל"ת" — he read a *resh* where the word has a *dalet*; it is *shod aniyim*, the *plunder* of the poor: the creditor who squeezes debtors until he takes their land or goods at a discount. A pure text-critical hassagah.
- **10:6 — the "shiggayon" of loving God.** Rambam says love of God requires that one "err/be obsessed (*yishgeh*) in it constantly." Ravad: "זה השגיון לא ידענו לאי זה דבר כיון" — we do not know what he aimed at — and offers two readings of his own: a term of song (as in *shiggayon le-David*, Ps. 7:1), or distraction from one's affairs out of love.

## The shape of the whole

Read together, the hassagot on Hilkhot Teshuvah are not random sniping. Ravad presses one consistent complaint in the doctrinal glosses: Rambam legislates philosophy — makes metaphysical positions (incorporeality, a bodiless world to come, an unanswerable account of foreknowledge) into binding doctrine with olam ha-ba stakes — where Ravad thinks the tradition left latitude, and where honest readers of Tanakh and aggadah could land elsewhere. Where the disagreement is practical (2:5) or textual (4:4), it turns on ordinary halakhic and philological grounds. The sources disagree and stay disagreed; the standard printed editions carry both voices on the page, which is itself the tradition's verdict on the exchange.

One caveat from the fetch: Ravad wrote no hassagah on chapters 1, 7, or 9 — so on the mechanics of teshuvah itself (confession's formula, the greatness of the penitent) the two are silent partners, not opponents.
