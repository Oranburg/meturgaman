# Baseline, question 1

Asked 2026-08-06, before any change to the agent. The question, verbatim:

> What does Jewish law say about lending at interest, and does it apply to a business loan?

The agent's answer, unedited:

---

Here is what the sources say, each fetched with its edition named. Romanizations are SBL general style; no uncertainty flags fired.

## The Torah prohibition

Three separate passages prohibit interest between Jews:

- **Exodus 22:24** (Heb: Miqra according to the Masorah, CC-BY-SA; Eng: JPS Tanakh Gender-Sensitive Edition, CC-BY-NC): "If you lend money to My people, to the poor among you, do not act toward them as a creditor; exact no interest [*neshekh*] from them."
- **Leviticus 25:36** (same editions): "do not exact advance or accrued interest [*neshekh* and *tarbit*], but fear your God."
- **Deuteronomy 23:20–21** (same editions): interest is forbidden "whether in money or food or anything else" — but verse 21 permits it explicitly with a foreigner: "you may deduct interest from loans to foreigners. Do not deduct interest from loans to your fellow Israelites."

## How the rabbis frame it

- **Mishnah Bava Metzia 5:1** (Heb: Torat Emet 357, public domain; Eng: William Davidson Edition, CC-BY-NC) defines the two biblical terms: *neshekh* ("biting" interest — lending a *sela* to receive five *dinars*) and *tarbit* (increase in commodities). The rabbis then extended the prohibition well beyond fixed cash interest to *avaq ribbit*, "dust of interest" — the sugyot at Bava Metzia 60b–75b work through commodity futures, discounted prepayments, even non-monetary favors.
- **Mishnah Bava Metzia 5:11** (same editions): the prohibition binds every party to the transaction — "the lender, and the borrower, and the guarantor, and the witnesses. And the Rabbis say: also the scribe." This is unusual: the *borrower* sins too.
- **Shulchan Arukh, Yoreh De'ah 160:1** (Ashlei Ravrevei, Lemberg 1888, public domain) opens the laws of *ribbit* by stressing its severity — multiple negative commandments, with borrower, guarantor, and witnesses all transgressing. The Rema glosses there: **"there is no distinction between lending to a poor person or to a rich person"** (ve-ein chilluq bein im malveh le-'ani o le-'ashir).

## So does it apply to a business loan?

**Yes, in principle.** The Rema's gloss just quoted is the direct answer: the prohibition is not limited to charitable or subsistence lending. A straight interest-bearing loan from one Jew to another for commercial purposes is forbidden — to both parties.

**But the tradition built a compliant structure for exactly this case.** **Shulchan Arukh, Yoreh De'ah 177:2** (Lemberg 1888 edition) describes the *'isqa*: money given "half as loan, half as deposit" (*palga milveh u-falga piqqadon*). The recipient trades with it; the financier bears the risk on the deposit half and shares its profit, and must pay the recipient a wage for managing that half — otherwise the recipient's unpaid labor on the deposit would itself be disguised interest. This is a genuine profit-and-loss partnership, not a loan, so a return on it is not *ribbit*.

That structure was later standardized as the *heter 'isqa* document, which is how observant lenders, businesses, and Israeli banks handle interest-bearing instruments today. Two modern notes from the sources: **Kitzur Shulchan Arukh 65:28** (Heb: Torat Emet, public domain; Eng: Metsudah trans., CC-BY) rules that a gentile intermediary only removes the problem if the gentile bears the risk — so a "bank in the middle" does not automatically solve it; and contemporary halakhic literature (e.g., Warburg, *Rabbinic Authority II*, found via search) debates whether loans to a *corporation* need a heter 'isqa at all, since some authorities hold limited-liability entities fall outside personal *ribbit* — a live disagreement, not a settled point.

**Summary:** the prohibition on interest between Jews applies fully to business loans; lending at interest to non-Jews is biblically permitted (Deut. 23:21); and a business financing can be structured as an *'isqa* partnership (YD 177) — the basis of the modern heter 'isqa — so that a return is permitted profit-sharing rather than forbidden *ribbit*. For an actual transaction, a properly drafted heter 'isqa reviewed by a rabbi is the standard practice.
