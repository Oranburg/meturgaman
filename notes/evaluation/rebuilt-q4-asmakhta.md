> I keep seeing the word *asmakhta*. What is it doing?

The agent's answer, unedited:

---

*Asmakhta* (אַסְמַכְתָּא, Jastrow Dictionary, s.v. אַסְמַכְתָּא: from the root *s-m-kh*, "to lean, support") is doing one of two quite different jobs depending on where you meet it. Both are a kind of "leaning."

**1. In legal discussions: a promise nobody fully meant.**
An asmakhta is a conditional obligation — typically an exaggerated penalty clause — where the law presumes the promisor never seriously intended to pay, because he was leaning on his confidence that the condition would never occur. The recurring maxim is:

> אַסְמַכְתָּא לָא קָנְיָא — "an asmakhta does not effect acquisition"

This is actually disputed in the Talmud. Bava Batra 168a:14 (William Davidson Edition, Vocalized Aramaic, via Sefaria, CC-BY-NC) puts it as a tannaitic split: "Rabbi Yosei holds an asmakhta effects acquisition, and Rabbi Yehuda holds it does not"; the Davidson English there glosses asmakhta as "a transaction with inconclusive consent." The same dispute appears among the Amoraim at Bava Metzia 66a:11. The codified law follows the "does not acquire" view — Mishneh Torah, Sales 11:2 (via Sefaria) builds its conditional-sales rules on it — and Sanhedrin 24b extends the logic to gambling: one opinion disqualifies dice-players as witnesses because a stake won on asmakhta is quasi-theft (per Jastrow's summary of that sugya; I did not fetch that page).

**2. In exegetical discussions: a verse used as a peg, not a source.**
When the Gemara concludes about some law:

> מִדְּרַבָּנַן, וּקְרָא אַסְמַכְתָּא בְּעָלְמָא — "it is rabbinic, and the verse is a mere support"

(Moed Katan 3a:9, William Davidson Edition, Vocalized Aramaic, via Sefaria, CC-BY-NC; the identical formula recurs at Niddah 46b:7, Avodah Zarah 38a:1, and elsewhere), it means the law does not actually derive from the Torah. The verse was cited as a mnemonic hook or a mark of solemnity for a rabbinic enactment. How seriously to take these pegs became its own debate: the Kuzari 3:73 wonders whether the Sages had real interpretive traditions behind such citations, and the Maharal (Gur Aryeh on Shemot 19:15:2, Be'er HaGolah, Well 1:5, both via Sefaria) argues an asmakhta is never arbitrary — the rabbinic law genuinely grows out of the verse even if it is not d'oraita. Malbim (on Exodus 12:16:2) and Meshekh Chokhmah (Shoftim 27) offer their own theories. So the sources disagree about what an exegetical asmakhta *is*, not just where one occurs.

**Which one you're seeing:** if the surrounding discussion is about contracts, penalties, or acquisitions, it's sense 1; if it's about whether a law is biblical or rabbinic, it's sense 2.

One romanization note (SBL General Purpose): the romanizer raised `sheva-after-qamats` on קָנְיָא and בְּעָלְמָא and defaulted to vocal sheva ("qaneya," "be'alema"). In these Aramaic words the sheva is conventionally silent, so the received readings are *la kanya* and *be'alma* — the spellings I've used above.
