> Is there Jewish wisdom about limited liability?

The agent's answer, unedited:

---

Yes — and it is richer than a single proof-text. Halakhah's *default* is expansive liability (a loan binds both the person, *shiʿbud ha-guf*, and all his property, *shiʿbud nekhasim*), which is exactly why the tradition developed deliberate devices for limiting it, plus a floor of protections no creditor may pierce. Here is what the sources say, each fetched with its edition.

## 1. Apotiki meforash — the closest classical analogue

The debtor may designate one asset as the sole source of repayment. Shulchan Arukh, Choshen Mishpat 117:1 (Lemberg 1898 ed., public domain):

> העושה שדהו אפותיקי לב"ח... והוא שיכתוב להם מכאן תגבו
> "One who makes his field an *apotiki* for his creditor... provided he writes for them: *from this you shall collect*."

The commentaries (e.g., Me'irat Einayim on CM 118:8) define the explicit form by the formula לא יהא לך פרעון אלא מזה — "you shall have payment only from this." That is a contractual limitation of liability to a designated asset pool, which is structurally what a corporation is.

## 2. The iska — statutory risk-splitting in investment

Bava Metzia 104b:14 (William Davidson Edition, CC-BY-NC):

> אמרי נהרדעי: האי עיסקא פלגא מלוה ופלגא פקדון, עבוד רבנן מילתא דניחא ליה ללוה וניחא ליה למלוה
> "The Sages of Nehardea said: this *iska* is half loan and half deposit; the Sages instituted something agreeable to the borrower and agreeable to the lender."

Half the capital is a loan (the managing partner bears its risk and keeps its profit); half is a deposit (the investor bears its risk). Neither party carries unlimited exposure — the loss is partitioned by design. This construct underlies the *heter iska* used in Jewish commercial finance to this day.

## 3. A floor beneath the debtor that liability cannot reach

- **Deuteronomy 24:10–11** (Miqra according to the Masorah / JPS Gender-Sensitive, CC): the creditor "must not enter the house to seize the pledge" — collection is bounded by the debtor's dignity.
- **Mishneh Torah, Creditor and Debtor 1:7** (Torat Emet, public domain): מסדרין לבעל חוב — the court makes the debtor produce everything "down to the last needle," but then *returns* to him food, clothing, bedding, and (per the continuation of the halakhah) his craftsman's tools. Liability stops where subsistence begins.

## 4. Wisdom-literature suspicion of unlimited personal guarantees

Proverbs 22:26–27 (Miqra according to the Masorah / JPS, CC):

> אל תהי בתקעי כף בערבים משאות. אם אין לך לשלם למה יקח משכבך מתחתיך
> "Do not be one of those who give their hand, who stand surety for debts — lest your bed be taken from under you when you have no money to pay."

The *arev* (guarantor) who binds his whole person to another's debt is precisely what Proverbs warns against — a very old intuition that unbounded personal liability is folly.

## 5. The modern debate: is the corporate veil halakhically valid?

A contemporary treatment I found (A. Y. Warhaftig-adjacent literature in *Rabbinic Authority: The Vision and the Reality*, Urim 2013, ch. 7, "Piercing the Corporate Veil" — licence unknown, so I paraphrase rather than quote) reports that poskim including R. Shmuel Wosner and the Monsey beit din Machon Lehoro'ah ground corporate debt in *apotiki meforash*: the shareholders are not personally liable and the corporate assets alone answer for the debt. Others analyze it as *shiʿbud nekhasim* without *shiʿbud ha-guf* — property-liability with no personal obligation at all. The same literature debates when halakhah would itself pierce the veil (fraud, undercapitalization). The positions genuinely differ on the mechanism, though they broadly converge on recognizing the limitation.

**Honest caveat:** the tradition also pushes the other way — *shiʿbud nekhasim* lets a creditor chase sold land into a buyer's hands, and heirs answer for a father's debts from the estate. Limited liability in halakhah is never the presumption; it is a *negotiated* structure (apotiki, iska), bounded by a non-negotiable floor of debtor protections. That combination — freedom to contract for limited exposure, plus mandatory human minimums — is arguably the tradition's distinctive contribution to the topic.
